Payment Terms Explainer — What Do They Actually Mean?

How to Write a Professional Invoice That Gets Paid Faster

A professional invoice is more than a payment request — it is a legal document, a tax record, and a signal of how seriously you take your business. Research consistently shows that professionally formatted invoices with clear payment terms get paid faster than vague or incomplete ones. Here is what every invoice must include and why each element matters.

The 10 Required Elements of a Professional Invoice

  • Your business details: Full legal name or trading name, address, phone number, and email. If you have a logo, include it — it immediately makes the invoice look more professional and trustworthy.
  • Client billing details: The client's full name or business name, billing address, and the name of the person or department the invoice is addressed to. Sending to the wrong contact is one of the most common reasons invoices get delayed in large organisations — confirm the AP contact before starting work.
  • Unique invoice number: Every invoice must have a unique sequential number. Use a consistent format like INV-2026-001 or 001, 002, 003. Tax authorities use invoice numbers to audit completeness — gaps in the sequence can raise flags.
  • Invoice date: The date the invoice is issued. This starts the payment term clock.
  • Payment due date: Write the actual calendar date, not just "Net 30." "Due: July 1, 2026" creates urgency; "Net 30" allows clients to reset the clock. Always include both.
  • Itemised line items: Each service or product as a separate line with description, quantity or hours, unit rate, and line total. Be specific enough that a stranger could approve it without needing to contact you. "Consulting services — $2,500" invites questions. "Brand strategy workshop, 4hrs @ $375/hr — $1,500" does not.
  • Subtotal, tax, and total: Show the subtotal first, then any discounts, then tax with the rate explicitly shown (even if it is 0%), then the final total. Never combine these into a single number.
  • Payment methods and details: Include every accepted payment method with the full details needed to pay — bank account number and sort code for bank transfers, PayPal address, Stripe link. Friction kills payment speed.
  • Late payment terms: A clause like "1.5% per month on overdue balances" or a fixed fee creates a professional expectation. Only include it if you disclosed it before starting work.
  • Tax registration number: Required in many countries once you are VAT/GST registered. In the UK, your VAT number must appear on every VAT invoice. In Australia, your ABN must appear on all tax invoices.

The Single Most Important Invoicing Habit: Send Same Day

Invoice the same day you complete the work or deliver the goods. Every day you wait to send an invoice adds days to your payment timeline. A client who receives an invoice the day work is delivered is psychologically primed to pay — the work is fresh, the value is clear, and there is no ambiguity about what is being billed. An invoice sent three weeks later arrives cold, potentially after the client's next payment run, and with no connection to the fresh memory of the work delivered.

Invoice Payment Terms Explained

Payment terms define when an invoice is due and set expectations for both parties. Choosing the right payment terms for your business type, client relationship, and cash flow needs is one of the most impactful decisions you can make for your financial health. Here is a complete breakdown of the most common payment terms and when to use each.

Due on Receipt

Payment is expected as soon as the client receives the invoice. This is the most aggressive payment term and works best for: one-time clients you do not have an established relationship with, small project amounts where a short delay would be disproportionately impactful, and any situation where you have completed all deliverables upfront. Be aware that many corporate accounts payable departments cannot process same-day payments regardless of terms — for large company clients, Due on Receipt often creates friction rather than speed.

Net 7, Net 15, Net 30, Net 60, Net 90

Net 30 is the most common standard for B2B invoicing — payment is due 30 calendar days from the invoice date. Net 15 is common for smaller freelance amounts or where faster cash flow is critical. Net 60 and Net 90 are typically dictated by large enterprise clients and should be factored into your pricing — if you wait 90 days for payment, you are effectively providing an interest-free loan. "Net 30" means 30 calendar days from the invoice date — always write the actual due date alongside the term to remove ambiguity.

2/10 Net 30 (Early Payment Discount)

This term offers a 2% discount if payment is made within 10 days, with the full amount due within 30 days. It is an effective way to incentivise faster payment without demanding it. For clients with cash-rich accounts payable departments, a 2% discount for paying 20 days early is worth roughly 37% annualised return — many finance teams will take it. The cost to you is 2% of the invoice in exchange for dramatically faster cash flow.

Milestone Payments

For large projects, structuring payments around deliverables rather than calendar dates protects both parties. A typical structure is 30–50% upfront before work begins, a second payment at a mid-project milestone, and the final balance on delivery. Milestone invoicing reduces your exposure to non-payment, aligns your client's cash outflow with visible progress, and gives you capital to fund work in progress.

Retainer Invoices

A monthly retainer is a fixed recurring fee invoiced at the same time each month, usually the first business day. Retainers are the gold standard for service providers because they create predictable cash flow, simplify the invoicing process, and align the client relationship around ongoing value rather than individual deliverables. If you do regular recurring work for a client, propose moving to a retainer — it benefits both parties.

Invoice Mistakes That Delay Payment

Most delayed payments are caused by avoidable invoice errors. Here are the most common mistakes and exactly how to fix them:

  • Wrong or missing client details: Sending an invoice to the wrong entity, wrong address, or wrong contact is one of the top reasons invoices get rejected by AP departments. Confirm the legal billing name, billing address, and AP contact before starting any project.
  • Vague line item descriptions: "Consulting services — $2,500" gives the client's AP team no context to approve without checking. Every line item should be specific enough to be approved independently.
  • Relative due dates only: "Net 30" without a specific date allows the client to interpret when the 30 days starts. Always include the specific due date as a calendar date.
  • Missing payment details: If you accept bank transfer but do not include the account number, sort code, and reference, you have just created a mandatory back-and-forth before payment can happen.
  • Calculation errors: Even minor arithmetic errors cause invoices to be held for review. Double-check every total before sending. Our generator calculates everything automatically to eliminate this risk.
  • Sending late: The longer you wait to send an invoice after completing work, the longer you will wait to receive payment. Invoice same-day, every time.

Following Up on Overdue Invoices

Late payments are a normal part of business — but how you handle them matters. Send a polite reminder the day after the due date with the original invoice re-attached. If unpaid after 7 days, send a firmer follow-up citing the overdue amount and any late fees in your terms. After 14 days, consider a phone call or formal letter. For invoices over 30 days overdue, you have the right to charge statutory interest (UK: 8% over base rate under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts Act) or pursue via small claims court for amounts under your jurisdiction's threshold.
