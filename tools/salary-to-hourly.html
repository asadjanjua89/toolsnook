How to Convert Salary to Hourly Rate

Converting a salary to an hourly rate is one of the most useful calculations for anyone comparing job offers, considering a move to contract work, or trying to understand the real value of their compensation. The formula is straightforward but the inputs matter significantly — changing just one variable like weekly hours or vacation days can shift your effective hourly rate by several dollars.

The Basic Formula

Hourly Rate = Annual Salary ÷ (Hours per Week × Working Weeks per Year)

For the most common scenario — a full-time employee working 40 hours per week, 52 weeks per year — the denominator is 2,080 hours. A $52,000 salary divided by 2,080 gives exactly $25.00 per hour. A $60,000 salary gives $28.85 per hour. A $100,000 salary gives $48.08 per hour.

Daily Rate = Annual Salary ÷ Working Days per Year (typically 260)
Weekly Rate = Annual Salary ÷ Working Weeks per Year
Monthly Rate = Annual Salary ÷ 12

Why Vacation Days Change Your Effective Rate

For salaried employees with paid vacation, vacation days do not reduce your annual pay — you earn the same whether you take 0 or 30 days of leave. But they do reduce the total hours you actually work, which means each working hour is effectively worth more. A $52,000 salary with 10 vacation days means you work 2,040 hours (not 2,080), making your effective hourly rate $25.49 rather than $25.00.

This distinction becomes critically important when comparing a salaried role with generous vacation to a contract role with no paid leave. The contractor earning the same gross hourly rate as the employee is actually earning significantly less on an equivalent basis once unpaid vacation, sick leave, and public holidays are factored in.

Salary vs Contractor Rate: What You Actually Need to Charge

One of the most common questions from professionals considering contract or freelance work is: "If I earn $X as an employee, what should I charge as a contractor to come out the same?" The answer is consistently higher than most people expect — typically 1.5× to 2× the equivalent employee rate.

The Hidden Costs of Employment That Contractors Pay Themselves

When you are an employee, your employer covers costs that are invisible to you but significant in real terms. When you become a contractor, these all become your responsibility:

  • Employer payroll taxes: In the US, employers pay 7.65% of your salary toward Social Security and Medicare. As a self-employed contractor, you pay both the employee and employer portions — a total of 15.3% on your net earnings.
  • Pension or 401(k) contributions: Many employers match pension contributions up to 3–6% of salary. As a contractor, any pension savings come entirely from your own income.
  • Paid vacation and sick leave: A salaried employee with 20 days paid vacation is effectively getting 20/260 = 7.7% of their salary as paid time off. A contractor with no paid leave loses that entire amount.
  • Health insurance: Employer-sponsored health insurance can be worth $5,000–$15,000 per year in total premium value, depending on coverage.
  • Accounting and admin: Self-employed professionals typically spend $500–$2,000 per year on accountants, software subscriptions, and business admin costs that salaried employees do not incur.

The Contractor Rate Formula

A commonly used starting point is to take your desired equivalent annual income, add back the cost of benefits and taxes you will now pay yourself, then divide by your billable hours (not total hours, since contractors typically spend 20–30% of their time on unbillable work). Our calculator shows your suggested contractor daily rate at 1.5× your salary equivalent — a conservative starting point for negotiation.

Part-Time Salary Conversion

Converting a part-time salary to an hourly rate uses the same formula but with different inputs. A £30,000 salary working 30 hours per week (rather than 37.5) gives: £30,000 ÷ (30 × 52) = £19.23 per hour. Compare this to a full-time colleague earning the same £30,000 at 37.5 hours: £30,000 ÷ (37.5 × 52) = £15.38 per hour. The part-time worker has a higher hourly rate for the same annual salary — which is the correct framing when comparing roles with different hours.

Salary Conversion for Job Offers and Negotiations

Salary to hourly conversion is an essential tool when evaluating job offers, negotiating compensation, or comparing roles with different structures. Here is how to use it effectively in each context.

Comparing Two Job Offers

When two offers have different salary levels but also different vacation allowances, working hours, or benefits, you cannot compare them by annual salary alone. Convert both to an effective hourly rate using the same inputs (hours per week, vacation days) and compare those. An offer of $70,000 with 30 days vacation and 35 hours per week may have a higher effective hourly rate than $80,000 with 10 days vacation and 50 hours per week.

Negotiating a Salary Increase

When preparing for a salary negotiation, knowing your current hourly rate and the hourly rate your target salary implies can sharpen the conversation. "I am currently earning $28.85 per hour and the industry median for this role is $36 per hour" is a more specific framing than "I want a $15,000 raise." It anchors the conversation in market data rather than a round number. Pair this with our comparison table to see where your rate sits relative to common benchmarks.

Evaluating a Promotion

If a promotion comes with a title change and salary increase but also significantly more hours, convert both the current and new roles to hourly rates to see whether the effective increase is as large as the headline suggests. A 20% salary increase that comes with 25% more hours is actually a slight pay cut on an hourly basis.
